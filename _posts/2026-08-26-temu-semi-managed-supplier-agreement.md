# TEMU Semi-Managed Supplier Agreements: 8 Clauses That Decide Your Margin

TEMU's semi-managed model looks simple on the surface. The seller ships bulk inventory to TEMU's US warehouse. TEMU handles listing, pricing, promotion, fulfillment, and customer service. The seller gets paid per unit sold minus platform fees and a platform-controlled discount.

What is not simple is the supplier agreement. The standard contract runs 30-50 pages and contains clauses that look boilerplate but materially change the economics. Sellers who sign without reading the agreement routinely discover — months later — that the margin they projected is 30-50 percent smaller than what they actually receive.

This article walks through the eight clauses that drive the real economics of a TEMU semi-managed supplier relationship. None of them are the ones the seller typically reads first.

## How Semi-Managed Economics Actually Work

In the semi-managed model, the seller is the supplier. TEMU is the retailer. The seller ships inventory to TEMU's bonded US warehouse under a pre-arranged purchase order. TEMU lists the product, sets the retail price, runs the promotions, fulfills the order, handles returns, and pays the supplier on a settlement cycle.

The supplier's revenue per unit is:

```
Net_Payout = Wholesale_Price - Platform_Fee - Settlement_Deductions - Return_Reserve
```

Where:

- **Wholesale_Price** is the price TEMU agrees to pay the supplier per unit. It is typically 40-60 percent of the eventual retail price.

- **Platform_Fee** is TEMU's commission, which ranges from 5-15 percent of retail depending on category.

- **Settlement_Deductions** include promotional subsidies, return deductions, customer service deductions, and warehouse handling fees — most of which come out of the supplier's payout.

- **Return_Reserve** is a percentage held back from each payout to cover returns. Typical reserves are 5-10 percent and are refunded after the return window closes.

The clauses below determine each of these components.

---

## Clause 1: Wholesale Price Adjustment Mechanism

Most supplier agreements contain a clause allowing TEMU to unilaterally adjust the wholesale price based on "market conditions," "competitive positioning," or "category benchmarks." The clause is rarely triggered in the first 90 days. After 90 days, it is routinely triggered.

What to watch for:

- Adjustment trigger conditions (specific market events, specific competitor price points, specific inventory thresholds)
- Notice period before adjustment takes effect
- Right of supplier to refuse adjustment (rare, but exists in some contracts)
- Floor price protection (a minimum wholesale price regardless of adjustment)

A supplier shipping $50,000/month at a 50 percent wholesale-to-retail ratio who experiences a 10 percent unilateral price adjustment loses $5,000/month. The clause deserves attention.

---

## Clause 2: Promotional Subsidy Allocation

TEMU runs aggressive promotions. The cost of those promotions is split between TEMU and the supplier. The split ratio varies by category, season, and the supplier's negotiating position.

What to watch for:

- Default subsidy split (typically 50/50, but varies 30/70 to 70/30)
- Maximum subsidy exposure per month or per quarter
- Right of supplier to opt out of specific promotional events
- Notice period before promotional subsidy is applied
- Mechanism for disputing subsidy charges after the fact

A supplier on the default 50/50 split during a major promotional period (Memorial Day, Black Friday, year-end) can see 20-30 percent of their monthly payout consumed by promotional subsidies. The clause does not flag this — the deductions appear in the settlement statement.

---

## Clause 3: Return Handling and Deduction

Returns on TEMU are handled by the platform. The supplier bears the cost — both the refund (deducted from payout) and the inventory disposition (return-to-seller, destroy, or liquidate).

What to watch for:

- Default return rate assumption used to calculate the reserve
- Process for disputing returns that are clearly abuse (empty box, used item, wrong item)
- Cost split for return shipping (supplier vs platform)
- Inventory disposition election right (right to demand return-to-seller instead of destruction)
- Time limit on returns (returns accepted within how many days of delivery)

The return rate assumption matters more than the other clauses for low-AOV items. A 15 percent return rate on a $5 item produces a reserve that wipes out margin entirely if the reserve is set at 10 percent and the actual return rate is higher.

---

## Clause 4: Inventory Liability After Warehouse Receipt

Once inventory arrives at TEMU's US warehouse, the liability structure changes. The supplier no longer owns the inventory in the legal sense — TEMU does, under a consignment or purchase-on-demand model. The exact ownership transfer point affects what happens if the warehouse burns down, if TEMU goes bankrupt, or if the inventory is recalled.

What to watch for:

- Ownership transfer point (at shipment, at receipt, at sale, or never)
- Insurance responsibility between shipping and warehouse receipt
- Insurance responsibility during warehouse storage
- Recall procedure and cost allocation
- Force majeure exceptions (which events excuse TEMU's obligations)

A supplier shipping $200,000 of inventory who experiences a warehouse incident with a contract that transfers ownership at shipment has limited recourse. The same supplier with ownership transferring only at sale has a stronger position.

---

## Clause 5: Settlement Cycle and Payment Terms

The settlement cycle determines how long the supplier waits between sale and cash. Most agreements run weekly, bi-weekly, or monthly settlement with payment terms of T+7 to T+30 from settlement close.

What to watch for:

- Settlement frequency (weekly vs monthly)
- Payment terms (days from settlement close)
- Minimum payout threshold (small balances carried forward)
- Currency conversion responsibility and rate
- Reserve release schedule (how long until return reserve is paid out)

For a supplier with thin margins and high volume, settlement timing is a working capital question. A T+30 monthly cycle on $200,000/month revenue means $200,000 of permanent working capital tied up in the platform. T+7 weekly on the same volume ties up $50,000.

---

## Clause 6: Exclusivity and Channel Restrictions

Many TEMU semi-managed agreements contain restrictions on the supplier selling the same SKU on competing platforms at lower prices. The "lowest price" or "price parity" clause is standard.

What to watch for:

- Exclusivity scope (specific SKU, product category, all categories)
- Price parity requirement (matching TEMU's retail, matching the lowest competitor, or both)
- Penalty for breach (deductions, suspension, termination)
- Right to delist from TEMU without penalty if the supplier wants to exit

Price parity clauses are the most expensive clauses to violate. A supplier offering the same SKU at $1 lower on another platform can be terminated from TEMU, losing the entire US sales channel overnight.

---

## Clause 7: Quality and Compliance Liability

TEMU enforces quality standards through the supplier agreement. Defective products, products that fail compliance testing, and products that generate customer complaints can result in chargebacks against the supplier.

What to watch for:

- Defect rate threshold above which chargebacks apply
- Quality inspection responsibility (supplier self-certify vs TEMU inspect)
- Compliance documentation requirement (CE, FCC, CPSC, etc.)
- Chargeback formula for defects
- Dispute resolution process

A defect rate above 3 percent typically triggers chargebacks. For a high-volume supplier, even a 1 percent unexpected defect rate produces meaningful chargeback exposure.

---

## Clause 8: Termination and Inventory Recall

The termination clause determines what happens to inventory in the TEMU warehouse when the relationship ends. The recall clause determines the process and cost of getting the inventory back.

What to watch for:

- Termination notice period required from either side
- Inventory recall process (who pays shipping, who arranges freight, timeline)
- Final settlement timing after termination
- Inventory that TEMU can liquidate, destroy, or hold pending supplier pickup
- Penalty for early termination by supplier

A supplier whose agreement allows TEMU to liquidate remaining inventory at TEMU's discretion after termination has no protection against TEMU clearing warehouse space at the supplier's expense. The clause matters most when the relationship ends badly.

## What It Does Well

- **Time.** A 50-page contract reviewed clause-by-clause against market practice takes a senior manager half a day. The MCP review takes 5 minutes.

- **Reproducible.** The same eight-clause framework applies to every TEMU semi-managed agreement. Re-run the prompt for every contract.

- **Open source.** The MCP server is open source on GitHub. The clause framework is plain English. The seller can adjust it.

- **Where it runs.** Claude Code, Codex, Cursor — the same AI agent environment the seller uses for listing and keyword work.

---

## Where It Falls Short

It does not replace legal review. The prompt identifies clauses that need attention. The actual contract language interpretation and enforceability assessment should go to a lawyer with marketplace expertise.

It depends on agreement text quality. If the contract is provided as a scanned PDF, the prompt cannot parse it cleanly. OCR or manual text extraction is needed first.

It benchmarks against publicly known market practice, not the seller's specific negotiation leverage. A supplier with strong volume and category position has more leverage than a first-time seller. The prompt does not weight this.
